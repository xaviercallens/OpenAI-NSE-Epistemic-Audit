\section{Introduction}
\texttt{u/Little-Name9809} asked whether the OpenAI construction suggests a way to drive a liquid to extreme states using \emph{only mechanical force}. Working through the question gives a sharper result than the one originally anticipated: for a liquid at ordinary pressure, the first physical assumption of the incompressible model to fail is not incompressibility, not the continuum hypothesis, and not viscous heating, but the liquid's inability to sustain tension. Cavitation comes first, and by a wide margin.

\section{Hydrodynamic Cavitation}
In the OpenAI proof, the collapsing core has velocity $|u(\tau)| \sim \tau^{-1/2-h}$ with $h = 1/200$, and radius $\ell_r \sim \tau^{1/2}$. Because the core keeps a radial Reynolds number of order one, these scales are diffusive in any units, $\ell_r \simeq \sqrt{\nu t}$ and $u \simeq \sqrt{\nu/t}$, essentially independent of the initial vortex size. The static pressure at the centre of a vortex lies below the ambient pressure by roughly the dynamic pressure, $\Delta p \sim \rho u^2$ (exactly $\rho u_{\max}^2$ for a Rankine vortex). The local cavitation number
\begin{equation}
    \sigma(\tau) = \frac{p_\infty - p_v}{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho |u(\tau)|^2}
\end{equation}
therefore falls to order one when $\rho u^2 \sim p_\infty - p_v$.

For ordinary water containing nucleation sites, cavitation sets in at $u \approx 14$\,m/s, about $5$\,ns before the mathematical blow-up, when the core is still $\approx 70$\,nm across: three orders of magnitude in time before the Mach limit and while the continuum description is unquestionably valid. Even the metastable tensile limits of very clean water are reached before, or together with, $\mathrm{Ma} = 0.3$. The Bernoulli pressure drop, not thermal shock, is the mechanism that removes a real liquid from the incompressible model.

\paragraph{Update (17 September 2026, v5.5.0): the pressure coefficient.} The table uses the cavitation number with $\tfrac12\rho u^2$. For a Gaussian (Lamb--Oseen) core the central pressure deficit is exact: $\Delta p = (\ln 2/0.638^2)\,\rho u_{\max}^2 = 1.70\,\rho u_{\max}^2$. With it, cavitation in water with nuclei sets in at $u\approx7.6$\,m/s, $\approx17$\,ns before blow-up, when $\ell_r\approx130$\,nm. The construction's profile is not exactly Gaussian, so the two rows bracket the threshold rather than fix it; the ordering of the limits is unchanged. Once a vapour core opens, a hollow vortex caps the swirl speed near $\sqrt{2(p_\infty-p_v)/\rho}\approx14$\,m/s. These are estimates; a cavitating-liquid simulation of the forced core has not been done.

\paragraph{Caveats.} The core at these times is tens of nanometres wide and lives for nanoseconds, so nucleation kinetics (the number of nuclei in a $70$\,nm region, the time to grow a critical bubble) matter and can delay cavitation toward the tensile-strength rows of the table. The comparison with the thermal estimate of Work Stream~2 is consistent: viscous heating gives $\Delta T = u^2/c_p$, which is only $48$\,K at $\mathrm{Ma} = 0.3$; there is no ``plasma by pure force''---temperatures of order $10^4$\,K would require $\mathrm{Ma} \gtrsim 4$, far outside the model's validity.

\paragraph{Speculative remark.} One might ask whether the violent collapse of the resulting cavity could concentrate energy in the manner of sonoluminescence. We record this as an unsupported analogy only: nothing in the present analysis establishes it, and the construction provides no blueprint for it.
